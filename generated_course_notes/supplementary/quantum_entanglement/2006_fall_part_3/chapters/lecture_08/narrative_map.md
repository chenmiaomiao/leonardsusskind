# Narrative Map

## Lecture Arc

The opening Maxwell question establishes the method for everything that follows: symmetry sharply restricts a theory but does not determine it without assumptions such as linearity and experimental input. Wave superposition and the nonlinear oscillator make that distinction concrete.

The lecture then rebuilds geometry from the interval. Mixed units, oblique axes, and uneven coordinate grids reveal the metric as a local quadratic form while warning that complicated coefficients need not mean curvature. Polar coordinates provide the decisive flat example; the sphere then supplies genuine intrinsic curvature.

With that distinction secure, the argument moves to spacetime. Large-scale homogeneity and spatial flatness motivate comoving labels and the metric
\[
d\tau^2=dt^2-a^2(t)(dx^2+dy^2+dz^2).
\]
Questions about filaments, simultaneity, bound systems, and whether expansion is a force remain attached to the assumptions they test.

For fixed comoving separation, physical distance is \(D=a\Delta x\). Differentiation gives \(V=(\dot a/a)D\) and defines the Hubble parameter. The null condition gives \(dx=dt/a(t)\), separating the changing coordinate speed from the invariant local speed of light.

The second half asks what observations and dynamics can add. Luminosity and redshift motivate distance and velocity measurements. A constant-velocity backward extrapolation gives the Hubble time \(H^{-1}\), while gravity explains why this is only an age scale. Exchanges about entropy, shrinking rulers, coordinate uniqueness, and time-varying \(H\) refine the interpretation.

Newton's shell theorem then reduces the homogeneous many-galaxy problem to the radial motion of one test galaxy under the enclosed mass. The calculation of \(a(t)\) is deliberately postponed.

The late questions complete the conceptual picture. Light propagation depends on the full history of \(a(t)\); the Big Bang is homogeneous expansion rather than an explosion into a shell; fixed \(G\) can coexist with changing forces; and spatial curvature is independent of whether the scale factor grows or shrinks. The lecture ends at the escape-velocity question that launches Lecture 9.

## Rhythm to Preserve

1. Begin with the limits of symmetry before introducing geometry.
2. Let awkward coordinates earn the metric rather than presenting it as a definition without motivation.
3. Use polar coordinates before the sphere so variable coefficients are not mistaken for curvature.
4. Keep spatial flatness distinct from four-dimensional spacetime flatness.
5. Derive the Hubble law directly from physical distance.
6. Preserve the correction from constant H to constant recession velocity in the age estimate.
7. Keep all classroom questions beside the claims they test.
8. Stop the Newtonian calculation at the shell-theorem setup.
9. Retain the late horizon, Big Bang, gravity, and curvature exchanges as part of the lecture.
10. End with the scale-factor dynamics problem, not a detached summary.
