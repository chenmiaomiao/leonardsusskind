# Chapter Plan

## Lecture Arc

The lecture begins with trusted Newtonian language, isolates gravity's
universal acceleration in Galileo's flat-Earth approximation, then repairs
that approximation through the inverse-square law and tidal gradients. The
second half changes language from forces to fields and from local derivatives
to global flux. Spherical symmetry finally turns Gauss's theorem into
Newton's shell theorem and points toward the relativistic exterior problem.

## Editorial Decisions

- Preserve the slow opening through frames, vectors, coordinates, and
  conserved mass because it prepares the cancellation in free fall.
- Keep the falling cloud, optical-detection question, external-floor
  distinction, and support-force explanation as separate logical steps.
- Retain the laboratory comparison that demonstrates the smallness of
  Newton's constant.
- Derive Kepler's period law rather than merely asserting that Kepler
  motivated the inverse-square force.
- Resolve the live many-body sign correction by defining the displacement
  vector explicitly.
- Distinguish instantaneous force superposition from nonlinear coupled
  evolution.
- Present tidal acceleration through the field gradient and supply the
  standard radial and transverse eigenvalues.
- Preserve the water analogy together with its compressibility limitation.
- Translate the lecture's generic spherical source into the standard
  Newtonian equation involving mass density.
- Preserve both limitations raised at the end: the mine-shaft interior and
  the hollow-shell cancellation.

## Classroom Exchanges

Ten focused question-and-answer blocks preserve the lecture's questions on
optical detectability, felt acceleration, equal forces, short-distance
physics, many-body evolution, tides, sign conventions, compressibility,
radial vector direction, and the shell interior.
