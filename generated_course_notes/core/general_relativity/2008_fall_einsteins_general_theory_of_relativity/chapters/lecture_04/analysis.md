# Lecture 4 Source Analysis

## Central Argument

The lecture has two deliberate halves. The first completes the algebraic
minimum needed for geometry:

1. Define vectors and tensors by their coordinate transformation laws.
2. Contract upper and lower indices to create lower-rank tensors.
3. Derive the metric transformation law from the scalar interval.
4. Introduce the inverse metric and raise or lower indices.
5. Use the invariance of the zero tensor to justify tensor equations.

The second half applies the same language to spacetime:

1. Characterize Cartesian transformations by the Euclidean interval.
2. Replace Euclidean distance by Lorentzian proper time.
3. Introduce the general spacetime metric and Minkowski signature.
4. Read a time-dependent scale factor as cosmological expansion.
5. Compare that time dependence with plane, sphere, and horn metrics.

## Source Corrections

- A metric is invertible because it is nondegenerate, not because symmetry
  alone guarantees an inverse.
- Covariant components are metric duals of vector components; in a general
  coordinate basis they are inner products with basis vectors rather than
  naive orthogonal projections.
- The identity tensor is written \(\delta^{m}{}_{n}\).
- The withdrawn board equality in the displacement contraction is not
  retained.
- Arbitrary coordinates may mix space and time, but a nonsingular
  transformation preserves Lorentzian signature.
- The cosmological metric is normalized consistently as
  \(g_{ij}=-a^{2}(t)\delta_{ij}\).
- A rotationally symmetric surface is written
  \(ds^{2}=dr^{2}+f^{2}(r)d\theta^{2}\), so the circumference is
  \(2\pi f(r)\).
