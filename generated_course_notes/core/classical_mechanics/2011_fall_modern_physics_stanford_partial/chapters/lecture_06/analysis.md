# Lecture Analysis

## Mathematical Spine

The lecture develops Hamiltonian mechanics in one continuous argument:

1. reversible discrete maps motivate complete states and information
   preservation;
2. position alone is enlarged to canonical phase space;
3. the Legendre transformation replaces velocity by momentum;
4. varying the transformed function produces Hamilton's equations;
5. the ordinary particle verifies equivalence with Newtonian mechanics;
6. constant-energy contours give the geometric picture of the flow;
7. Poisson brackets express time evolution and conservation in one formula.

## Editorial Decisions

- The chapter follows the lecture chronology rather than rearranging the
  material into a later textbook taxonomy.
- All 17 substantive audience exchanges are retained at their original
  conceptual locations.
- The opening red-dot argument is refined carefully: reversibility motivates
  information preservation, while canonical Hamiltonian structure supplies
  phase-volume preservation.
- The complementary-area construction is stated with its normalization and
  monotonicity assumptions.
- Singular Legendre maps are not called physically impossible. Ordinary
  inversion fails, while constrained systems require a separate formalism.
- Reduced friction is distinguished from Hamiltonian dynamics of the enlarged
  system containing environmental or field degrees of freedom.
- The cubic Hamiltonian is retained as the lecture's nonlinear example, with
  an explicit warning that it has no globally regular ordinary Lagrangian.
- The convention
  \[
  \{A,B\}=\sum_i(A_{q_i}B_{p_i}-A_{p_i}B_{q_i})
  \]
  is used consistently with \(\dot A=\{A,H\}\).
- Corrupted automatic-caption wording in the late bracket discussion is
  reconstructed only where the surrounding derivation is unambiguous.
- Opening and closing institutional announcements are omitted.
