# Narrative Map

## Opening

Begin at the widest scale: action is a common form of physical law, not merely
an alternate technique for one mechanics problem. Narrow immediately to the
two calculus tools that make the global-to-local conversion possible.

## Movement

1. Derive the endpoint formula as a telescoping sum.
2. Derive integration by parts and pause for the \(dt\) question.
3. Isolate the endpoint-vanishing form needed later.
4. Prove the arbitrary-test-function lemma with a localized blip.
5. Contrast local evolution from initial data with a global boundary-value principle.
6. Deform the physical path while fixing both endpoints.
7. Define the action when the audience asks for it, preserving the lecture's order.
8. Differentiate the action, include the sum over coordinates, and move the derivative by parts.
9. Use arbitrariness of the deformation to obtain Euler-Lagrange pointwise.
10. Name momentum and force only after the equation is established.
11. Descend through examples: one particle, many particles, two interacting particles.
12. Elevate the two-particle result into the symmetry-conservation principle.
13. Distinguish continuous translation from discrete inversion.
14. Break one of two translation symmetries with near-Earth gravity.
15. Defer energy conservation while preserving the audience question that motivates it.
16. Change to polar coordinates and end with rotational symmetry, angular momentum, and the ice-skater effect.

## Voice

- Teach the mathematics directly rather than reporting what a lecture or transcript says.
- Use first-person plural for calculations and geometric constructions.
- Preserve questions at the point where they alter the pacing.
- Let each equation answer a physical question before moving to the next abstraction.
- Keep provenance and correction language in audit files, not in the narrative body.

## Transitions

- The two calculus tools lead directly to the variational calculation.
- Fixed endpoints explain both the geometry of nearby histories and the vanished boundary term.
- Canonical terminology follows, rather than precedes, the Euler-Lagrange equation.
- Newton's equation establishes confidence before symmetry provides the deeper result.
- The external-lump example makes closed-system translation invariance concrete.
- Near-Earth motion shows what happens when only one coordinate is cyclic.
- Coordinate independence licenses the final polar-coordinate example.

## Closing

End on the lecture's own pattern, not on a detached summary:

\[
\text{translation invariance}\Rightarrow\text{linear momentum conservation},
\qquad
\text{rotational invariance}\Rightarrow\text{angular momentum conservation}.
\]

The final sentence reconnects those local conservation equations to
stationarity of the entire history.
