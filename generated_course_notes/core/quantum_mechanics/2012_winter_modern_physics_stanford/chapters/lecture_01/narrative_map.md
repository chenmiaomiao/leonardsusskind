# Narrative Map

## Opening

The audience and course format establish the governing promise: reach the
basic ideas quickly, but use enough mathematics to get them right. That
promise explains why the lecture begins with logic and experiments rather
than a postulate list.

## Physical Arc

1. A moon receiving random classical kicks becomes unpredictable and loses
   exact energy conservation.
2. A quantum system may have unpredictable outcomes while preserving a sharp
   energy under fixed closed dynamics.
3. The two-slit experiment defeats the classical sum of alternatives, even
   for particles sent one at a time.
4. Reversible two- and three-state maps turn information conservation into a
   forward-then-backward test.
5. Classical random deviations ruin that test; undisturbed quantum evolution
   does not.
6. Intermediate detection and environmental path records change what can
   interfere.
7. The plate-recoil question leads naturally to momentum uncertainty.
8. Heisenberg's microscope connects spatial resolution to photon momentum
   and unavoidable back-action.
9. Two gentle, widely separated position measurements recover velocity only
   by relinquishing precise position.

## Mathematical Arc

1. A classical pure state is a point in phase space; a classical probability
   density represents incomplete knowledge about that point.
2. Quantum states require a space closed under complex linear combinations.
3. Kets are abstract vectors, while ordinary spatial arrows are treated as
   the special real-vector example called pointers.
4. Complex functions and fixed-length complex columns satisfy the two linear
   closure rules.
5. Complex conjugation prepares the next step toward inner products,
   operators, Hermitian operators, and eigenvalues.

## Ending

The chapter does not add a retrospective summary. It ends where the lecture
ends: complex conjugation as a map, followed by the promise of operators,
Hermitian operators, eigenvalues, and applications.
