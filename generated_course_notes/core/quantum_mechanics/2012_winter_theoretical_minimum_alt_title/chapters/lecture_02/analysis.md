# Lecture 2 Source Analysis

## Central Problem

A spin carries directional information, but an analyzer along any axis reports only \(+1\) or \(-1\). Classical geometry returns only at the ensemble level:

\[
\langle \sigma_{\hat m}\rangle=\hat n\cdot\hat m.
\]

The lecture uses that tension to motivate two departures from classical description. Sequential tests of incompatible propositions are order-dependent, and physical states must be represented in a complex linear space rather than as elements of an ordinary set.

## Narrative Arc

1. Rename the qubit a spin and distinguish spatial three-vectors from abstract state vectors.
2. Introduce an oriented two-outcome analyzer, repeatability, and measurement as preparation.
3. Compare discrete single events with vector-like ensemble averages.
4. Demonstrate the failure of classical set logic with \(A:\sigma_z=+1\) and \(B:\sigma_x=+1\).
5. Review complex vector spaces, inner products, norms, orthogonality, and dimension.
6. Construct the up--down, right--left, and in--out bases in \(\mathbb C^2\).
7. Interpret orthogonality as distinguishability and remove normalization and global phase from the physical parameter count.
8. Defer the rigorous state--observable relation to the next lecture.

## Source Cautions

- The arrow is explicitly provisional; it must not be presented as a classical microscopic spin direction.
- Repeating one measurement on one prepared spin is not the same experiment as gathering statistics from repeated identical preparations.
- The expected-value discussion is colloquial. The reliable statement is that angle brackets denote the arithmetic mean over trials.
- The Born-rule wording is garbled, but its squared-magnitude content is unambiguous in context.
- The in/out construction and its change of basis are live calculations with hesitations; only the final consistent algebra is retained.
- The relationship to three-dimensional rotations, Pauli matrices, and gauge invariance is announced but not developed.
