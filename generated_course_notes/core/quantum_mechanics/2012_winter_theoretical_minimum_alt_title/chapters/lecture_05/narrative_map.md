# Narrative Map

## 00:00:06 to 00:14:18

The lecture moves from the dimensional ladder of simple systems to repeatable measurement. A joint measurement must leave a state definite for both quantities, so a complete common eigenbasis leads directly to commuting operators. The converse is stated, Pauli components supply the counterexample, and a student asks for the common-basis argument again.

## 00:14:22 to 00:31:58

The discussion returns to dynamics. Preservation of orthogonality becomes unitarity; continuity near zero time gives an infinitesimal generator; unitarity makes that generator Hermitian. The resulting state equation is definite between interventions even though measurements remain probabilistic. The energy eigenvalue problem is then separated from the time-dependent equation.

## 00:32:08 to 00:52:28

Expectation values are introduced explicitly as averages. Differentiating the bra-ket sandwich gives the commutator equation. The lecture then studies antisymmetry and the product rule, compares commutators with Poisson brackets, restores hbar dimensionally, and derives conservation of average energy. Questions clarify the generic meaning of $L$ and commuting observables on distinct subsystems.

## 00:52:28 to 01:18:37

Once $H$ has been diagonalized, an arbitrary state is expanded in its energy basis. Each coefficient obeys an independent exponential phase equation. Inserting the result into an expectation value produces the matrix formula with energy-difference frequencies. The ensuing questions preserve coefficient magnitudes, derivative notation, the rigidity of unitarity, real energies, and the no-summation convention.

## 01:18:37 to 01:43:01

A magnetic field finally makes the one-spin Hamiltonian nontrivial. The dipole argument motivates $H=\omega\sigma_z/2$. Direct matrix multiplication gives the Pauli commutators, and their factors of $i$ produce real equations in which the transverse averages rotate while the field-aligned average stays fixed.

## 01:43:03 to 02:03:40

The field is treated provisionally as a heavy classical apparatus. The Hamiltonian is generalized to $(\omega/2)\bm\sigma\cdot\bm n$, with direction in $\bm n$ and magnitude in $\omega$. Trace and determinant fix the two energy levels. The closing exchange distinguishes weak radiative decay from precession and explains why a sideways superposition produces a full photon with a probability, not a photon with half the energy.
