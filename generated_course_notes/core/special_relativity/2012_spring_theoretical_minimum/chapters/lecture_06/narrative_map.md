# Narrative Map

## Opening Setup

The lecture begins as a continuation of the previous notation lesson. The transition to electrodynamics is deliberately delayed: indices must first become reliable enough that electric and magnetic forces can later appear as one covariant statement. The chapter therefore keeps the notation review as an argument about economy and invariance, not as a detached glossary.

## Beat Sequence

1. Upper and lower vector components are related by the mostly-plus Minkowski metric. The component signs lead naturally to contraction and the summation convention.
2. The scalar differential shows why differentiation with respect to an upper-index coordinate produces a lower-index gradient. Four-divergence supplies a second compact scalar.
3. Lorentz transformations are written as matrices. A boost mixes time with one spatial direction, while a spatial rotation leaves time fixed.
4. Covectors require the dual transformation matrix. A live index correction and the relation between the two matrices reinforce that free indices must match.
5. Products of vectors motivate rank-two tensors. One matrix acts on each index, and the rule extends to arbitrary rank whether or not the tensor factorizes.
6. A component-level audience question expands one rank-two output into sixteen terms. Tensor equality then explains why covariant equations remain true in every inertial frame.
7. Raising and lowering are extended to tensors, followed by questions about dimensionality and geometric meaning. The lecture postpones the deeper vector-covector geometry to general relativity.
8. Symmetric and antisymmetric tensors are contrasted. Vanishing diagonal entries leave six independent antisymmetric components, exactly enough for three electric and three magnetic components.
9. The four-potential is introduced, and the worldline contraction with a displacement gives the simplest local scalar interaction for a charged particle.
10. The invariant action is rewritten in ordinary time. The Euler-Lagrange calculation then proceeds slowly through canonical momentum, spatial derivatives, the total derivative along a moving path, and the grouping of velocity-independent and velocity-dependent terms.
11. After the completed calculation, the class asks why this interaction was chosen, whether Maxwell equations also follow from an action, where the sums occur, and why the velocity-independent combination deserves the name electric field.
12. Proper time turns the spatial momentum equation into a four-vector equation. The fourth component is interpreted as the work-energy law.
13. The lecture closes by extracting three principles from the calculation: locality, Lorentz invariance, and gauge invariance. The first two have already been used; the third is reserved for the electromagnetic field action.

## Transition Cues

- The opening promise that notation will make later formulas simpler motivates the tensor review.
- The move from products of vectors to transformation laws changes an example into a definition.
- Six independent antisymmetric components provide the bridge from tensors to electromagnetism.
- The phrase that there is no way around the algebra marks the long Euler-Lagrange calculation.
- Grouping by velocity dependence changes a page of derivatives into electric and magnetic physics.
- Multiplication by dt over d tau is the decisive step from component mechanics to covariance.
- The final principles turn one particle calculation into a template for relativistic field theory.

## Pacing Risks

- Compressing the notation review into definitions would remove the reason each index rule is introduced.
- Jumping from the action to the Lorentz force would erase the main mathematical work of the lecture.
- Moving the late audience questions beside the earlier action would break the recorded classroom rhythm.
- Treating the fourth equation as a formal appendage would lose its work-energy interpretation.
- Expanding gauge invariance here would overrun the source; the lecture only announces it.
